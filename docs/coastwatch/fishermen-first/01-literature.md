## 0. Corrections to the existing synthesis (read first)

1. **S2 (Moore S.K. 2020), "only 26% thought their coping actions were sufficient."** The full text does not support this number. What it says is: "CA participants were most likely to answer 'no' (27% for both Fish and Non-fish) compared with WA and OR" [L3, §3.3]. That is the share who said their actions were **not** sufficient. The share who said "yes" in California is not stated in the text, which points to Fig. 6. **Replace the S2 wording.**
2. **S2, which help California fishing participants wanted.** The paper's text contradicts itself. One passage says that for Fish participants "tax relief (… 59.5% in … CA) and increased government services (… 41.8% in … CA) were thought to be the most helpful." The next sentence says that "in CA they thought that financial support (46.8%) and late payment penalty waivers for taxes (41.8%) would help" [L3, §3.4]. Quote both passages, or cite Fig. 7 directly. Do not cite only one of them.
3. **S31 (Fisher 2021), "roughly two-thirds of vessels stopped fishing temporarily."** This figure comes from press summaries. The paper itself gives **56.4 ± 16.7%** of northern-region Dungeness crab vessels and **72.5 ± 0.1%** of central-region crab vessels "discontinued all fishing in California" during the early season [L6, pp. 3–4]. Use the paper's figures.
4. **Disaster-aid timing (S2, S5: "more than three years" / "June 2018").** June 2018 is when funds were **allocated or announced**. The money reached fishermen later:
   - PSMFC announced it had received the funds on 22 May 2019, and the paperwork deadline was 31 July 2019 [L28, UNVERIFIED snippet].
   - Pomeroy & Culver 2026 says funds reached participants "in late 2019, 4 years after the fishery closures" [L18].
   - Corrected chain: request 9 Feb 2016 → determination 18 Jan 2017 → allocation of about $25.8M in June 2018 → checks in roughly mid-to-late 2019 [L26, L28].

## 2. Findings by question

### Q1. HAB closures and unpredictable openings; income instability; lost opportunities

- **[R] Catch and revenue are front-loaded, so delays remove the most valuable weeks.**
  - Catches "are concentrated in the first six weeks after openings and drop off steeply" [L5, p. 2].
  - The California fishery is a "limited-entry derby fishery" in which "the majority of crab is landed during the first 6 weeks of a 7-month season" [L7, p. 5–6, citing Dewees et al. 2004].
  - For whale closures, "vessels earned about 10.5% of annual revenue after April 1" [L8].
- **[R] The scale of the 2015–16 delay.**
  - Central district opened 26 March 2016, "roughly 4.5 months late."
  - Northern district (except Eureka) opened 12 May.
  - Eureka opened 26 May, "50 days after the central district opened" [L7, pp. 7–8].
  - California landings were "52% of average catches and 58% of average revenue the prior five years" [L5, p. 2].
- **[R] The delay cost fishers revenue outside crab too.**
  - Modeled total revenue for the crab fleet would have been **$26.1M higher** without the delay. Dungeness revenue alone would have been **$13.6M higher**.
  - So "over half of the estimated loss … is attributable to reduced revenues in other fisheries" [L5, p. 8].
  - Direct harvest-sector income loss was **$18.12M** and processing **$3.29M**; indirect losses were of "similar magnitude" [L5, p. 9].
  - Ex-vessel prices fell **22–23%** (Mao & Jardine 2020, cited in L5, p. 11).
- **[R] Income shock at the household level (2017 survey).**
  - The median income decrease for California fishing participants was **$10,000–$99,999**, versus $3,000–$9,999 in WA and OR.
  - California and Oregon fishing participants recouped losses less often than non-fish participants (**25.0% vs 39.8%**) [L3, §3.2].
  - For a typical California fishing-license owner, the predicted probability of high income loss (>$10,000) was **0.85** [L4].
  - California closures lasted "up to 72% of the Dungeness crab season" versus 12–13% in OR and WA [L4].
- **[R] Delays are now chronic, driven mostly by whale and turtle risk, sometimes by domoic acid.**
  - Commercial openers were delayed in every season from 2022–23 to 2025–26 [L11].
    - 2023–24: opened 18 Jan 2024 (Zones 3–6) under a 50% gear reduction.
    - 2024–25: opened 5 Jan 2025 (Zones 3–6, 50%) and 15 Jan (Zones 1–2, 25%).
    - 2025–26: opened 5 Jan 2026 (Zones 3–5, 40%). Zones 1–2 were delayed further "pending testing."
  - Press: the 2024 delay was the "sixth straight year" (Lookout, 21 Nov 2024) or the "sixth consecutive season" [L30]. The traditional opener is 15 Nov.
- **[R] Unpredictable reopening, with short notice, in the 2025–26 north** [L36, L37, UNVERIFIED snippets]:
  - CDFW first saw elevated DA "as early as late September" 2025 in pre-season testing [L36].
  - The delay of BMA B (Reading Rock–Cape Mendocino) was announced **8 Jan 2026, 7 days** before the planned 15 Jan opener.
  - OEHHA cleared the area 23 Jan. It opened **30 Jan** with a **64-hour** gear-setting window from 27 Jan.
  - Vessels that had fished elsewhere could not fish there until **1 March** (fair start) [L37].
- **[R] HAB closures also hit the rock crab fishery.** The year-round rock crab fishery "had never before been closed" until 2015, when it "was closed in two-thirds of the state" [L18, §1].
- **[T] Fishermen describe a total loss of predictability:**
  - "You used to be able to depend on the opening of crab season like clockwork… It sure doesn't feel that way anymore… It's really hard to know what to do." [L35]
  - Ogg's "crystal ball was broken a long time ago" (paraphrase-quote) [L30].
- **[H]** The unpredictability of the opening date, not only its lateness, drives costs such as crew retention, buyer contracts and the decision to travel. Only the loss side is quantified. No study measures the cost of uncertainty itself; Seary et al. call for considering "timing of closures" [L8].

### Q2. Cost pressure: fuel, maintenance, equipment, insurance, slip fees

- **[R] Fuel.**
  - Fuel is "a major component of variable costs for Dungeness crab harvesters" (Dewees et al. 2004, cited in L7, p. 16).
  - Large vessels travelling to distant openings likely raised their fuel costs. So revenue is not profit, and the cost side is **not measured** in any HAB study I found [L7, p. 16].
- **[R] Fixed costs persist through closures.**
  - Pomeroy & Culver note the "ability to cover fixed costs such as vessel payments, insurance and slip fees" as a concern.
  - "Fishermen highlighted the potential deferral or reduction of slip fees" [L18, §3.1.1, §3.2.4].
- **[R] Slip fees: local relief exists but is uneven.**
  - The Port of San Francisco approved Resolution 24-29 (June 2024), a five-month waiver (15 May–15 Oct 2024) for ~50 salmon boats (combined ~$14,912/month) and ~30 crab gear-storage tenants (~$8,921/month).
    - Revenue impact to the Port: ~$119,167.
    - Stated reason: "many of the Port's salmon and crab fishers have been pushed to the economic brink of collapse" [L21].
  - At the same time, San Mateo County Harbor District proposed a 3.7% fee increase for commercial users, which drew objections [L22, L23].
- **[T] Operating costs while prices fell:** "in 2024, crab prices to vessels hit their lowest point in 20 years, while fuel, bait and other operating expenses were at an all-time high." The same letter notes vessels "were able to use only half of their allocated (and paid for) gear" [L22]. This is an association letter, not audited data.
- **[T] Bills continue during closures** (2016, Mendocino): crew rely on "credit or dwindling savings while insurance, mortgages, and other bills continue"; "many fishers are paying mortgages on the boats." This is the reporter's paraphrase of fishers [L31].
- **[T] Deferred maintenance.** A San Francisco salmon fisher's century-old boat "needed deferred maintenance" and her permit was "four years out of date" (reporter's paraphrase) [L24].
- **[R] Fishing at a loss to keep crew:** 56% of West Coast vessel owners "have fished when profits might not cover vessel expenses in order to employ crew" [L15].
- **[U] Rising insurance premiums for California crab boats.** This is commonly asserted, but I found **no California or West Coast data** for 2020–2026. The only recent premium evidence is from Alaska: "My insurance on my boat has gone up… 30% in the last two years" (Kodiak fisherman, KMXT, Dec 2024), which is [E-adj] at most. CalMatters reports "high insurance costs for new crew" deter mentoring, but gives no figures [L25].
- **[R] Coping by debt:** written-in coping actions for fishing participants included "going into debt (i.e., living off credit cards)… and using retirement or other savings" [L3, §3.3].

### Q3. Small vs large vessel constraints

- **[R] Definitions:** "small vessels defined as less than 40 feet"; large is ≥40 ft [L7, p. 4, 10; L6 Methods].
- **[R] Small vessels lost revenue share and participation share in several ports.**
  - Average marginal effect (with full controls): the small-vessel revenue share fell by about **0.14** (2015/16) and **0.18** (2016/17). The participation share fell by about **0.07** and **0.12** [L7, §4.2].
  - The 2015/16 revenue effect is "a 30% reduction from the before-period mean proportion (0.14/0.455)."
  - Port-level revenue-share effects were significantly negative for **Crescent City, Eureka and Bodega Bay** in both HAB seasons, and for **San Francisco and Monterey** in 2016/17.
  - Crescent City had "impacts over two times the average impact, in both HAB seasons" [L7, §4.2].
- **[R] Mechanism: mobility.**
  - "Large vessels had a greater ability to mitigate losses."
  - Some large vessels routinely work two port areas, such as Crescent City/San Francisco and Eureka/San Francisco, while "no small vessels" show that pattern [L7, §4.1].
  - In 2015/16, Eureka-based large vessels cut landings in Eureka by ~40% and increased them in San Francisco by ~33%. Small vessels shifted about half as much (−20%/+16%).
  - Six candidate pathways are listed: season compression, spillover, exploiting short fair-start windows, the 2016/17 Bodega delay "interpreted as a signal," the unprotected Bodega opening, and the incremental Fort Bragg openings [L7, pp. 8–9].
  - Fisher et al.: "Landing catch in a different community… was mostly undertaken by large vessels." "Fishers with smaller vessels instead relied on alternative fisheries to remain active in-place" [L6, pp. 3, 5–6].
- **[R] Dropout was similar across sizes.** "Early season vessel dropout was relatively consistent between large (≥40 ft) and small (<40 ft) vessels" [L6, p. 3]. Size predicts **mobility**, not whether a vessel stops fishing.
- **[R] Whale closures.** In the Central Management Area, "small vessels were estimated to have incurred larger percentage losses," while large vessels had larger dollar losses [L8].
- **[R] Disaster pay favoured larger permits.**
  - 2015–16 relief was paid by pot-limit tier: about $42,68[0] for Tier 1 down to $14,983 for Tier 7, "about $85 per pot." The paper prints "$42,68", a typo.
  - Because "vessels with higher pot limits tend to be larger, … larger vessels would have tended to get higher disaster assistance payouts though they may have been better able to avoid losses" [L5, p. 12].
- **[R] Policy response.** Fair-start law (FGC §8279.1) was amended to apply to vessels rather than persons (1 Jan 2017), and in 2018 to "any delayed area" [L7, p. 7 fn. 1, p. 7]. Fisher et al. note these amendments "may limit the feasibility of spatial redistribution" [L6, p. 6].
- **[T, secondary]** "fishers reported that the larger and most mobile vessels were able to participate in multiple openings" (citing Press Democrat 2017) [L7, p. 10].

### Q4. Dependence on particular fisheries and ports; diversification and portfolios

- **[R] Dependence on Dungeness.**
  - Dungeness is "~26% of all annual fishery revenue" in California and "supports >25% of all commercial fishing vessels" [L6, p. 1].
  - In a permit-holder survey, "over 80% of respondents stated that over 60% of their gross income came from fishing Dungeness crab" (Dewees et al. 2004, cited in L7, p. 6). These are 2004 data.
- **[R] The "gilded trap" in the north.**
  - Northern networks (Crescent City, Eureka, Fort Bragg, Bodega Bay) were "composed of fewer fisheries; more highly centralized around Dungeness crab."
  - During the closure, northern fisheries connectivity fell **58%**.
  - Approximately **87% and 84%** of active small and large vessels "concentrated participation in a single alternative fishery," mainly sablefish and rockfish/lingcod [L6, p. 3].
  - Dungeness crab may be "a seasonal 'gilded trap' for northern California fishing communities" [L6].
- **[R] Diversification helps, but only if the substitute is open and permitted.**
  - "Most fishers did not have an alternative fishery… because nearly all fisheries are limited access and relatively few crabbers had permits for other fisheries occurring in the winter" [L5, p. 10].
  - Alternate fishing raised the probability of income recovery by **0.28** for fishers. Advertising raised it by **0.40**, but advertising and side jobs raised stress [L4].
- **[R] Salmon closure analogue.** In 2008–09, vessels "more dependent on salmon were more likely to cease fishing while more diversified vessels were more likely to continue" [L13, abstract]. Stayers in four California fisheries had higher diversification, income stability and income [L14].
- **[R] The 2023–2025 salmon closure compounds the crab problem.**
  - Commercial salmon was closed in 2023 and 2024. The 2025 status differs by source: "canceled for three years in a row" [L24] versus a brief recreational opening. CalMatters says recreational fishing was "barred for all but a handful of days in 2025."
  - Commercial salmon reopened **16 May 2026** with trip limits and an 83,000-fish quota (search summary of CDFW/OPC; UNVERIFIED detail).
  - The SF Port memo: the fleet "has suffered a double hit" [L21].
- **[T]** "When you take away Dungeness crab season, you take away 100% of their ability to make a living… Without that one fishery, they have nothing to fall back on." Kenny Belov, restaurant co-owner who oversees two crab boats [L33].
- **[T]** "We've had a reduction in our crab season, a reduction in our rockfish season, and now we are going to go nine months without potential income." Dick Ogg, Bodega Bay. The quote is reproduced in a public comment; the original outlet is UNVERIFIED [L23].
- **[T]** "Nothing's going to replace salmon." Matt Juanes, salmon and crab fisher [L34].

### Q5. Whale entanglement / RAMP restrictions and their effects

- **[R] The framework.**
  - RAMP is in 14 CCR §132.8, effective 1 Nov 2020. RAMP 2.0 amendments were approved by OAL and effective **21 Oct 2025** (file 2025-0910-01SR), after an OAL disapproval on 23 May 2025 [L11, L12].
  - CDFW assesses risk for humpback whales, blue whales and leatherback turtles, and acts by **Fishing Zones 1–6**.
  - The action menu: fleet advisory; gear reductions of 15/25/40/50%; a **30-fathom depth constraint**; delay; zone closure; recreational trap prohibition; and Alternative ("pop-up") Gear authorization.
  - Electronic monitoring is required from 2025–26 [L11]. Purple/black line marking is required from 2025–26 (search summary; UNVERIFIED).
- **[R] How actions are announced.**
  - Each assessment produces a **Director's Declaration**, posted with a CDFW Final Assessment, a Working Group recommendation and a preliminary data document.
  - There is a "Whale Safe Fisheries email list" [L11].
  - The page shows **no text-message or hotline channel** for receiving updates; 1-877-SOS-WHALE is for reporting entanglements.
  - Lead time between declaration and effect varies by action: 13 Mar → 27 Mar 2026 (14 days); 17 Apr → 30 Apr 2026 (13 days); 19 Dec 2025 → 5 Jan 2026 opener [L11].
  - The regulatory minimum notice period could **not be verified**, because the reg text was blocked [UNVERIFIED].
- **[R] Recent seasons, end-loaded closures** [L11]:
  - **2022–23:** Zones 3–6 closed 15 Apr 2023. A 30-fathom constraint in Zones 1–2 from 15 May. Zones 1–2 closed 15 Jul.
  - **2023–24:** Zones 3–6 closed after 28 Mar 2024. A 30-fathom constraint in Zones 1–2 from 8 Apr.
  - **2024–25:** Zones 4–6 closed 15 Apr 2025. Zone 3 closed 1 May. A 30-fathom constraint in Zones 1–3. Zones 1–2 closed 20 Jun.
  - **2025–26:** Zones 4–5 closed 27 Mar 2026. Zone 3 closed 30 Apr with Alternative Gear from 7 May. A 30-fathom constraint in Zones 1–2 from 30 Apr. Zones 3–5 closed for the rest of the season to traditional gear, with Alternative Gear authorized.
- **[R] Cost of whale closures** (Central Management Area loss) [L8]:
  - **$9.37M** in 2019 (observed $15.01M versus predicted $24.38M).
  - **$14.43M** in 2020 (partly COVID: about 4.1% of the 2020 CMA loss attributed to COVID price effects).
  - Northern Management Area: $0.28M (2019) and $3.87M (2020, mostly meat-quality delay and COVID).
  - Uncertainty is large: out-of-sample errors ranged "from a $22 million loss to a $8 million gain."
- **[R] HAB and whale risks stack within a season.** The page notes that "HAB impacts that tend to hit the earlier part of the Dungeness crab season are being compounded by a recent rise in whale entanglements later in the season" [L1]. The heatwave turned "near win–win" into "win–lose" trade-offs [L9]. Season compression "may amplify these distributional impacts" [L7, p. 28].
- **[T]** "Every delay is difficult. Right now, I have zero income as a fisherman… we've lost all the holiday markets… The whales are doing fantastic. It's the commercial fishermen that are really becoming extinct." Shawn Chen Flading, San Francisco fisher and Gear Working Group member [L29].
- **[T]** "This is our primary income… It would be like if somebody told you they won't let you work for the next month and a half… The delay is very impactful both emotionally and financially." Dick Ogg, president, Bodega Bay Fisherman's Marketing Association [L30].
- **[T]** "This opening reflects a careful balance and shows what is possible through shared effort to thread the needle." Lisa Damrosch, executive director, PCFFA, 20 Dec 2025 [L38]. This shows that industry leadership also publicly supports RAMP compromises.
- **[R] Effects on fishermen beyond revenue are unstudied.** Effects such as stress, crew loss and gear-switching costs for pop-up gear and electronic monitoring are **not in peer-reviewed literature I found** [gap].

### Q6. Regulatory information fragmentation; communication failures; lab-to-published delays

- **[R] Fragmentation:** "advisories are posted on various websites, sent out to those who sign up to receive them"; participants asked for a "timely notification system — accessible, easily understood"; "decision-making has not been transparent" [L18, §3.2.1, §4, Table 3].
- **[R] Lab and result delays:** limited lab capacity means results may not be reported "in a timely manner, potentially further delaying access to the fishery." Testing is "costly ($100 per crab)" [L18, §3.2.1].
- **[R] Fishers asked for clearer communication.** "California fishers have requested more reliable and clear communication by scientific and regulating institutions during future HAB events" (citing Ritzman 2018) [L6, p. 6]. Early-warning effectiveness "varies by region" (citing Ekstrom 2020) [L6].
- **[R] Separate regimes with separate channels.**
  - DA status flows through CDPH/OEHHA → CDFW news and the Health Advisories page (S12).
  - Entanglement status flows through Director's Declarations on the Whale Safe Fisheries page and a dedicated email list [L11].
  - Fair start is statutory (FGC §8279.1) [L7].
  - A single opener can depend on all three, as in 2025–26 Zones 1–2, "delayed pending testing" [L11, L37].
- **[R] Speed from sample to decision in 2025–26** [L36, L37 snippets]:
  - Pre-season high DA in late September 2025.
  - Delay notice 8 Jan 2026.
  - Clearance 23 Jan, opening 30 Jan.
  - The record does not show when intermediate test results were published to fishermen [gap].
- **[R] Official documents go stale.** The FGC staff summary for the 13–16 Oct 2026 meeting reports the 31 Aug razor-clam reopening, while CDPH and CDFW closed the fishery again on 9 Oct [L32; S12].
- **[R] Disaster-aid process communication failures:** "inadequate communication about request status," and officials "would like more detailed information on the process" [L19].
- **[T]** "But they don't give us a 30-day notice, right?" A fisherman with permits in both crab fisheries [L18, §3.2.3].
- **[T, 2015]** "To date, we haven't gotten a formal response on letterhead." Ben Miller, Rep. Huffman's chief of staff, on the relief request [L31]. This is political staff, not a fisher.
- **[H]** For DA, the biggest information delay is probably between a CDPH result and its publication or decision, not between the decision and the announcement. **No study measures this interval.**

### Q7. Disaster assistance: difficulty and delay

- **[R] California Dungeness and rock crab 2015–16** [L26, L5, L3, L18, L28]:
  - Request 9 Feb 2016 → determination 18 Jan 2017 → $25,797,268 allocated (announced June 2018).
  - Funds received by PSMFC around May 2019, paperwork deadline 31 Jul 2019 [L28, UNVERIFIED].
  - Participants were paid in "late 2019, 4 years after the fishery closures" [L18].
  - About $14M went directly to commercial crab permit owners [L5, p. 11].
- **[R] Effectiveness.** Holland & Leonard: aid "may not be an effective way to ensure the financial viability of fishers… that do not have sufficient resources to weather at least a year of low income" [L5, p. 11]. Moore et al.: "Waiting for disaster assistance may therefore be an ineffective coping strategy" [L3, §4].
- **[R] California salmon 2023** [L20, L27]:
  - Request 6 Apr 2023 → determination 30 Oct 2023 → allocation 1 Feb 2024 ($20.6M) → checks mailed 11 Jul [2025, inferred; UNVERIFIED].
  - That is about **27 months** from request to checks.
  - Industry said in May 2024 that it had "not yet received the money" [L23].
- **[R] California salmon 2024:** request 12 Apr 2024 → determination 12 Dec 2024 → allocation 10 Jun 2026, "$21.3" (units not shown; presumably $M) [L20]. Payment to fishers is **not yet documented** [gap].

- **[T]** "it took years to get that money when the salmon got shut down." Scott Hockett, Noyo Fish Company, about 15 boats [L31].
- **[T]** "The entire framework for fishery disasters has to be totally redone." Rep. Jared Huffman [L24].

### Q8. North Coast vs Central vs Southern California

- **[R] North: most exposed to DA and most specialized in Dungeness.**
  - Northern networks were more centralized on Dungeness and less connected [L6].
  - Crescent City, Fort Bragg and Moss Landing are the most vulnerable (S6).
  - The longest 2015–16 closures were in Eureka and Crescent City [L7].
  - The 2025–26 DA delay hit BMA B (Reading Rock–Cape Mendocino) [L37].
  - DA problems at "Trinidad and Crescent City" also occurred in 2018 [L35].
- **[R] Central: more diversified, and the main locus of whale closures.**
  - Connectivity in central networks *rose* 32% during the closure [L6].
  - Dungeness vessels were only 14% of vessels in Monterey and Morro Bay [L6, Table 1].
  - RAMP closures since 2023 fall earliest on Zones 3–6 (Sonoma/Mendocino line southward) [L11].
  - Whale-closure losses concentrated in the CMA [L8].
- **[R] A caveat on the central region.** Central-region crab vessels *stopped fishing at a higher rate* in the early 2016 season (**72.5%**) than northern ones (**56.4%**) [L6, pp. 3–4]. So "central = resilient" holds at the **community network** level, not for the **individual crab vessel**.
- **[R] South: under-studied.** Pomeroy & Culver "did not include systematic input from fishery participants south of Santa Barbara County" [L18, §2.2]. No southern study of HAB livelihood impacts (rock crab, spiny lobster, CPFV) was found [gap]. Fisher's and Jardine's samples end at Morro Bay [L6, L7].
- **[H]** Southern California HAB exposure for commercial fishers runs mainly through rock crab viscera advisories (e.g., Northern Channel Islands, S12) and market perception, not Dungeness closures. This is untested.

### Q9. Connectivity, computer literacy and age of the fleet

- **[R] Age.** West Coast vessel owners (2017 survey, 55% Californian, N≈1,437, response rate >50%): "mean age of 58 years, with more than 50% over 60" [L15].
- **[R/T, secondary]** CalMatters reports the average captain age in California is **48**. No source is given, so this is UNVERIFIED and conflicts with L15, which measured owners, not captains [L25].
- **[R, E-adj] Graying.**
  - Oregon: "graying is perceived as occurring by members of the fishing industry, but is not a concern for community leaders" (interviews with 48 fishermen and 17 leaders) [L16].
  - West Coast groundfish trawl: most fishermen are "approaching ages where they may consider exiting," with "no comparable younger cohort" [L17, abstract only].
- **[R] Crew economics.** Using NOAA data, "median annual crew pay fell about a third, from $90,468 (2014) to $61,592 (2024)" [L25, figures not checked against NOAA directly].
- **[R, weak] Connectivity at sea.** The reporter says fishermen work "out of wifi and cellphone range," and one captain used **radio** to share fish locations [L25]. California commercial logbooks other than the CPFV logbook are reportedly paper-only (search summary; UNVERIFIED).
- **[U]** "Older fishermen don't use smartphones or apps." I found **no California or West Coast study** of commercial fishermen's digital literacy, smartphone use or connectivity. Do not assume it. The Oregon S10 study (fishers "rely heavily on real-time data") cuts the other way.
- **[H]** Low-bandwidth and offline-tolerant delivery matters at sea, and SMS or email reach older users. Both need testing.

### Q10. Trust in official data vs local knowledge

- **[R] Existing evidence, reconfirmed.**
  - Fishers trust colleagues most, then Sea Grant and county staff. California fishing-industry respondents rated management effectiveness lowest (S3).
  - Interviewees say "decision-making has not been transparent" [L18].
- **[R] Evidence of social friction around sampling:** "pressure to not provide samples for testing was evident in some places" [L18, §3.2.1]. Because the monitoring depends on volunteered crab, distrust can reduce the data the system runs on.
- **[R] Whale data vs fishermen's observations** (search snippets of OPC working-group documents, UNVERIFIED):
  - In the 2017 pilot, whale-watch operators reported high humpback and blue whale concentrations, while "fishermen on the Working Group… were not seeing high numbers of whales." An aerial survey followed.
  - A Working Group summary from 2018 asked for data that are "verifiable and reliable" and for "more consistent aerial surveys and communications with the fleet."
  - TNC and the California Coast Crab Association now run a fishermen-led whale survey program to fill RAMP data gaps (2024 recommendations report).
- **[T]** "We are the true conservationists… We care about this water" (Ogg) [L30]. "We as a commercial fleet feel very comfortable doing our best practices to limit the interactions" (Flading) [L29].
- **[H]** Fishermen may trust *official status* (it is binding) while distrusting the *reasoning and data* behind it. An information product that exposes the underlying data and process may be valued for that reason (consistent with L18's call for transparency). Untested.

### Q11. Temporary inactivity vs permanent exit (see also §4)

- Strong evidence of **temporary** inactivity during HAB closures [R]: L6, L3, L5. Moderate evidence of **permanent exit** after salmon closures [R]: L13, L14. **No study links permanent exit or retirement to HABs.** See §4.

## 4. Participation and exit: what the evidence does and does not show

**Distinguish four outcomes.** Each source measures a different thing. Do not merge them.

| Outcome | Best evidence | What it shows | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Temporary inactivity during a closure** | Fisher 2021 [L6] | 56.4 ± 16.7% (north) and 72.5 ± 0.1% (central) of crab vessels landed nothing anywhere in California during the early 2016 season. Most "waited out the closures in port." Participation patterns "mostly returned to their predisturbance state" afterwards. | [R] |
| | Moore S.K. 2020 [L3] | Most common response among California fishing participants: no coping action (55.3%). Written-in responses: debt, savings, retirement savings. | [R] |
| | Holland & Leonard 2020 [L5] | "Some crabbers did not fish at all in 2016, giving up both crab revenue and revenue from other fisheries." | [R] |
| | Testimony | "It was more just, sit and wait for the date to go." [L18] | [T] |
| **Exit from crab into other fisheries (spillover)** | Fisher 2021; Bland 2021 / Jardine et al. 2024 [L6, L39] | Northern spillover mostly went to sablefish and rockfish/lingcod (16 alternatives in all). Past participation diversity predicted exit *from the crab fishery*. Vessels that left crab for other fisheries lost more revenue than those that stayed (thesis abstract). | [R] (L39 abstract only) |
| **Relative decline of small vessels** | Jardine 2020 [L7] | The small-vessel *share* of participants fell by 0.07–0.12. This is a share, not a count of exits, and could reflect larger-vessel entry. | [R] |
| **Permanent exit from all commercial fishing** | Richerson & Holland 2017 [L13] | **2008–09 salmon closure, not HAB.** 209 vessels (17%) had no commercial landings in 2008–2015 ("leavers"). More salmon-dependent and lower-revenue vessels were more likely to leave. | [R] |
| | Hackett et al. 2015 [L14] | **Restricted-access and quota events, not HAB** (Dungeness 1995, spot prawn 2002, sheephead 2001, spiny lobster 1996). Stayers had more diversification, more income stability and higher income. | [R] |
| **Retirement, selling a vessel** | None for HABs | No study found that measures retirement or vessel sale caused by HABs or RAMP. | gap |

**Why people stop. What is evidenced vs assumed**

- **[R] Economic predictors of exit after a closure** are known: lower revenue, fewer years fished, higher dependence on the closed fishery, and lower diversification or stability [L13, L14]. These are vessel- or license-level correlates, **not reasons stated by fishers**.
- **[R] Many plausible drivers are not measured.** Hackett et al. say exit is also shaped by "age, health, and disability status; educational attainment… spousal income… outside work opportunities." They did not measure these [L14, p. 1]. Richerson & Holland model at vessel level; "data availability limits us" from modeling individual behaviour [L13].
- **[R] Fishers are reluctant to switch occupations.**
  - Education and training for alternative employment was the **least** popular option in the 2017 HAB survey [L3, §4].
  - Over 93% of West Coast vessel owners prefer fishing to an equal-paying job, and fewer than 35% would switch for up to 50% more pay [L15].
  - 31% have worked a non-fishing job in response to a fishery closure [L15]. That is temporary side work, not exit.
- **[R] Ritzman et al. 2018** (as reported by Moore 2020): residents wanted a "one stop shop" for career resources, and perceived "a lack of alternative employment opportunities" [L3, §4]. Indirect; full text unread.
- **[T, anecdotal] Exits described in news after the salmon closures:**
  - Boats "went to the crusher" and fishers "left California or the industry," with no numbers given [L24].
  - An apprentice took a city job because "he had a mortgage, he had to support his family" [L25].
  - Pomeroy & Culver: "Some captains and crew moved elsewhere to fish or took on shore-based work" [L18].
- **[U] "HABs are driving fishermen to retire or sell their boats."** **No study supports this.** The 2015–16 HAB studies find temporary dropout and a rebound in participation patterns [L6]. Permanent-exit figures come from the **salmon** closure [L13]. Do not attribute retirement or vessel sales to HABs in any product copy.
- **[H]** Cumulative stacking may raise exit risk among older, less diversified owner-operators in the north and centre: repeated RAMP delays since 2018–19, DA delays, the 2023–25 salmon closures, low crab prices, and the cost of new gear rules (pop-up gear, EM, line marking). This is plausible given L13, L14 and L15 (mean age 58), but **no study tests it**. A PacFIN stayer/leaver analysis for 2015–2026 would.

## 6. Implications for an information product (tiered)

These are statements about what a HAB information product like CoastWatch can and cannot do. Each implication is tied to evidence.

1. **[R-backed] Show the multi-regime gate per area and date, without merging regimes.** One opener can depend on DA (CDPH/OEHHA), RAMP (Director's Declaration), meat fill and fair start [L7, L11, L37]. Fishers lose money when they misjudge these.
   - The product should *link* each regime's official document and show its date, with RAMP Zone and BMA as separate geographies.
   - It should never compute "open" (consistent with existing N1).
2. **[R-backed] Treat timing and notice as the core value, not forecasts.**
   - Revenue is front-loaded [L5, L7, L8]. Announcements can come only 7–14 days ahead [L11, L37], and fishers complain about notice [L18].
   - The highest-value function is **fast, reliable change notification**: what changed, effective when, and the source link.
   - A forecast of opening dates remains out of scope (existing N6). It would also be unverifiable given the uncertainty in L8.
3. **[R-backed] Expose protocol state and evidence for transparency.** Transparency is the explicit ask [L18], and trust in management is low (S3). Showing CDPH sample dates and the remaining protocol steps serves this. Sample-donation pressure [L18] means "no recent sample" must stay prominent (existing P5).
4. **[R-backed] Design for small-boat, place-bound users first.**
   - Small vessels cannot exploit distant openings [L6, L7] and lose proportionally more [L7, L8]. Their decisions are about **local** status and **substitute fisheries in place**.
   - So: port-first views, and alternate-fishery status (sablefish, rockfish, rock crab) shown with official sources.
   - [H] Showing *all* open areas coastwide may disproportionately help mobile large vessels. This is a fairness consideration, not a reason to hide information.
5. **[R-backed] Staleness is an observed, routine failure.** Even an official Commission staff summary was out of date within 4 days of the meeting [L32]. Every item needs "as of" and "superseded by" handling (existing N11).
6. **[R-backed] A disaster-relief tracker is low-cost and high-relevance.**
   - Request → determination → allocation → spend plan → checks takes 1.3–4.8 years [L19]. Stakeholders cite "inadequate communication about request status" [L19], and fishers and associations track it themselves [L22, L23].
   - A neutral timeline linking NOAA determinations and PSMFC pages fits CoastWatch's "quote, link, timestamp" pattern. **[H]** It is in scope only if CoastWatch accepts non-HAB content (salmon, whale-related relief).
7. **[H] Delivery channel.** Owners average 58 years old with more than half over 60 [L15]. Work happens out of cell range [L25]. RAMP relies on email lists [L11].
   - Email and SMS digests plus a lightweight web page are likely safer than an app-only design.
   - **There is no California evidence on digital habits**, so this must be tested in interviews.
8. **[H] Positioning.** Industry leaders publicly support RAMP compromises [L38] while expressing hardship [L29, L30]. A product that sounds *against* the regulators, or that reads as conservation advocacy, risks rejection from both sides. Neutral relay of official actions is the defensible posture.
9. **[U → avoid] Do not claim CoastWatch reduces losses, prevents exits or saves income.** No evidence links information products to those outcomes for California fishers. Holland & Leonard and Free et al. argue that monitoring and forecasting could enable *finer-scale management* by agencies [L5; S7/L40]. That is a management benefit, not a user-side claim.
10. **[R-backed] Copy rules (in addition to N1–N14).**
    - Never attribute retirements or vessel sales to HABs (§4).
    - Never cite "two-thirds of vessels stopped fishing"; use 56.4%/72.5% with the source.
    - Distinguish whale closures from toxin closures in every label. They have different agencies, triggers and evidence.

## 7. Open questions and gaps

1. **Ritzman et al. 2018 full text** is still unread. Quotes and specifics on barriers to unemployment and assistance in Crescent City remain secondhand.
2. **No peer-reviewed analysis of 2018–2026 seasons** combining DA, RAMP and salmon closures for California fishers. That includes participation, exit and revenue by vessel size and port. Jardine et al. 2024 (L39) was not read.
3. **Permanent exit or retirement attributable to HABs or RAMP:** no study. The NOAA West Coast Fisheries Participation Survey (2017/2020/2023/2026) may hold intent-to-exit and closure-response items by state; its results tool was not queried.
4. **Cost data:** there is no California data on insurance premiums, slip-fee levels across harbors, or fuel share for crab vessels after 2004 (Dewees). Testimony only [L22].
5. **Digital access and literacy** of California commercial fishermen and harbor staff: no study.
6. **Notice and lag metrics:**
   - The interval from CDPH lab result to publication to OEHHA recommendation to CDFW declaration has never been measured.
   - RAMP regulatory minimum notice could not be verified (reg text blocked).
   - The OEHHA 8 Jan 2026 memo sample values (Trinidad Head, Sept 2025, "10 to 180 ppm, 50% exceedance") are UNVERIFIED.
7. **Recent disaster requests:** whether California sought determinations for the 2022–23 to 2025–26 Dungeness seasons, and the payout status of the 2024 salmon allocation (determined 12 Dec 2024, allocated 10 Jun 2026).
8. **Southern California:** HAB livelihood impacts on rock crab, lobster and CPFV are undocumented.
9. **First-person North Coast testimony for 2023–2026** was not found in accessible outlets. North Coast Journal, Lost Coast Outpost and the Del Norte Triplicate should be searched directly, or covered by interviews.
10. **Average fleet age:** 58 (owners, 2017 survey [L15]) vs 48 (captains, CalMatters with no source [L25]). Reconcile with CDFW licensing data.
